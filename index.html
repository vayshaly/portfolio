01

Reading our own landing page the way a buyer would

Landing page audit · Positioning · Written for the people who built the page

I reviewed the sales page for the product I work on, element by element, deliberately sitting in the buyer's position rather than the team's. Everything was ranked by what would stop someone purchasing, not by what was easiest to fix. A visible rendering bug came second. This came first.

Extract from the review

The page says 19 of 24 participants improved, and the distribution implies n=24. Your campaign proposal says 80 participants. Your emails have said 57 and 18.

I am buying a measurement product. If I find two different participant counts, I stop trusting the measurement, and the measurement is the only thing making you different from twenty other AI courses. This is the single biggest credibility risk on the page.

I sent it to the people who had produced those numbers, who are also the people I report to. That was the uncomfortable part and I think it was the useful part.

Positioning usually fails on inconsistency before it fails on wording. A buyer does not need to catch you being wrong, only being two different things at once.

02

A campaign that opened well and converted badly

Email copy · Offer design · Campaign diagnosis

A send to 968 contacts opened at 23 percent, which is a healthy number, and converted under 1 percent. The instinct in the room was to rewrite the copy. The copy was not the problem.

The email carried a single call to action that went straight to a purchase page. For someone who had heard of us ten seconds earlier there was no step between interest and paying. We replaced it with a free first day, changed nothing else of substance, and clicks went from under half a percent to roughly 4 percent on the next send.

I also run the segment work upstream of this. Reviewing a 71 contact target list, I found 25 explicit declines and no genuine buying interest in the remainder, and recommended dropping the segment rather than putting more volume through it. It was dropped and the list rebuilt.

A high open rate with no conversion is not a copy problem. It is usually a problem with what you are asking people to do next.

Turning a product into language someone can act on

Long-form product writing · Three audiences, three registers

I wrote the chapters of Disciplined AI, a ten day course that takes people with no technical background and leaves them able to work with AI without handing over their judgement. Ten days, ten minutes each, one idea at a time, where every lesson has to survive being read by someone who is tired and unconvinced.

I wrote Chapter 20 of The Generative Organization, a published book on how organisations actually work with AI rather than how they announce it. And I wrote and shipped The Perfumery Handbook, a technical reference on fragrance chemistry written for people who are not chemists.

books.genorg.ai

Machine learning, organisational behaviour and perfume chemistry. The subject changes, the job does not: find the one thing the reader needs to believe, and say it before anything else.

04

Specifying what a good answer looks like, before anyone writes one

Prompt system design · 100+ systems for a live product

I designed more than 100 prompt systems for the AI4Innovation toolkit, a methods product now live and in use. Each one names the task, states the constraints, and describes what an acceptable answer looks like, including what the model should refuse to do, rather than leaving intent to be inferred.

That turned out to be the same discipline as product messaging. In both cases you are deciding, in advance and in writing, what good output is. Most weak messaging is not badly written. It was never specified.
